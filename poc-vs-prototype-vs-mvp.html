PoC vs prototype vs MVP: what a hardware startup needs, and when

Three words founders hear constantly, what they mean for a physical product, and which one you need next.

By Shreyas P, 21 October 2026. 3-minute read.

Short answer: a proof of concept proves the core idea can work, usually on the bench. A prototype looks and works like the product, and it’s what investors usually want to hold. An MVP is a small batch that real customers use. Most hardware founders raising a pre-seed or seed round need a working prototype first.

Why hardware is different

A software team can ship an MVP in weeks and fix it after launch. In hardware, every iteration means new parts, new boards and a new enclosure, so each stage has a different job. Skipping one usually costs more than it saves.

Proof of concept (PoC)

Its job: retire the biggest technical risk. Can this sensor really detect what you need? Will the radio reach far enough? Does the battery last long enough?

  • Built with: dev boards, modules, breadboards and focused test firmware.
  • Who sees it: you, your engineer, sometimes technical advisors or a grant committee.
  • Done when: the key function works reliably on the bench, with test results you can share.

Prototype

Its job: show the product working. Early on, teams sometimes build a “looks-like” model and a “works-like” model separately. An investor-ready prototype is both in one device.

  • Built with: a custom PCB, production-style firmware, an enclosure, and an app or dashboard where needed.
  • Who sees it: investors, early customers, pilot partners.
  • Done when: someone outside the team can use it in a demo without you explaining around it.

Minimum viable product (MVP)

Its job: learn from real users. Will they use it every day, and will they pay?

  • Built with: a small batch of units, designed for reliability, with firmware updates in the field and a way to support users.
  • Who sees it: pilot and early paying customers.
  • Done when: users rely on it and you have the data to plan production.

After the MVP: EVT, DVT and PVT

Before mass production, manufacturers run three validation stages. Engineering validation (EVT) checks the design works. Design validation (DVT) checks it meets its requirements and can pass certification. Production validation (PVT) checks the factory can build it consistently. You don’t need these to raise a seed round, but investors may ask how you’ll get there.

What investors usually expect

Every investor is different, but as a rule of thumb:

  • Pre-seed: a proof of concept or early prototype, and a clear plan.
  • Seed: a working prototype, early user feedback, a unit cost estimate and a path to manufacturing.
  • Series A: an MVP or pilot with real users and data.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the PoC, then discovering the core technology doesn’t work after paying for a polished prototype.
  • Perfecting the enclosure before the electronics work.
  • Building every feature into the first prototype instead of one convincing demo.
  • Calling a prototype an MVP and putting it in customers’ hands before it’s reliable.
  • Ignoring unit cost and certification until an investor asks.

Which one do you need?

  • Not sure the idea can be built: a feasibility sprint, then a proof of concept.
  • Core technology proven, raising money: an investor-ready prototype.
  • Funded and ready for pilots: an MVP, built on the prototype’s design.
